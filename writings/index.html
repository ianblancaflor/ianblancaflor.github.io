    Convenience Store Woman by Sayaka Murata
    My rating: 4 of 5 stars

    Pretty light and breezy story read about Keiko, a 36 year old woman who has been working at the same konbini (convenience stores like Lawsons, FamilyMart, 7-11, etc., prevalent in the cities of Japan) for 18 years. As a child she learns that her actions and views don't align with societal norms in two incidents: when she picks up a dead bird in the park and proposes to cook it because her father likes yakitori, and when she breaks up a school fight by hitting one of them with a spade (which she later refers to as a shovel... memories really do fade over time). Once she turns 18, a konbini (named Smile Mart) begins construction at a train station near her, and she gets a job there as a sales attendant. Given a set of instructions, coworkers to learn from/imitate, and structure, she's stable and happy to play the part as a "normal person", a nebulous idea of a type of person her family and friends want her to be. At get-togethers held by her friend, her mutual friends take turns updating each other on the momentum of their lives, and inquiring Keiko about why she's "stuck" at a dead-end job, why she hasn't met a man and gotten married. As Smile Mart gets older and physically stays the same, so does she. Managers, coworkers, and regular customers all come and go, while she willingly continues to work there, happy and content with unknowingly going against the grain of family building and career climbing, an expectation placed on her by everyone but her coworkers in the store. During the time of this book, she's on Manager #8, and has a supervisor Mrs. Izumi (a woman in her mid 30s who Keiko decides to imitate for fashion) and coworker Sugawara (who's 24, plays bass in a band, and is the template for Keiko's speech and mannerisms). As the get-togethers occur, she pieces together an idea of what the normal societal expectations are, and figures she'll give it a shot. Her creepy incel ex-coworker Shihara moves in under the premise that she can tell everyone "a man is living with me" so long as he stays rent free, food included, hidden from the world. This vague proclamation of "someone is staying over" is welcomed by her friends and cohort, colored by the context of societal expectation. Satisfied with those results, she quits her job too, and for a month she spends her days waking up, eating (sometimes), applying for jobs (at the insistence of Shihara, who wants to recluse and remain dependent on her labor), and going to sleep. One day she stops by Smile Mart and accidentally lets it slip out that Shihara is staying with her, and despite her coworkers initial disdain for him, they're excited for her and ask her to come out drinking with them sometime. Then, when Shihara and Keiko are on their way to an interview at a temp agency, she stops by another konbini. Disheveled by its untidy appearance, she begins shelving items and reorganizing, at the delight of the workers there, who assume she's a higher up konbini manager due to her suit. Shihara confronts her to stop, and she stands up for herself, exclaiming that the structure of working at a konbini gives her life and she wouldn't have it any other way.

    I started reading this book at work and ended up finishing it the same day. This book feels like a companion piece to the movie Perfect Days, which follows the life of a public toilet cleaner in Tokyo who faces similar societal pressures and resignation from people who consider him a dead-ender. Really interesting exploration of labor, and whether or not the labor is broadly appreciated and treated as respectable. Convenience store workers, custodians, etc. interact with people everyday to make the act of Going Outside possible, and yet they're vastly under appreciated, underpaid, and overworked world wide. What it means to find fulfillment in work and structure has a different meaning for everyone. For some (like Keiko's older sister Miho), that means settling down with a husband and having a kid, while for Keiko that means having the structured work at the konbini doing a "dead-end job"; This book posits the question about whether either of those things are actually "superior" to one another, and proposes that both have their unique benefits and consequences that unfold as the story does. The default scripts of social life work for most people, but for someone like Keiko, who is content with part time work at a konbini and is well-adjusted, why is one treated as an outcast?
